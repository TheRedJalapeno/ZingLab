If you've ever tried to drop a bit from a phone repair kit into a regular screwdriver, you know how it goes: it rattles around and falls out. That isn't you doing it wrong. They're two different sizes.

## Two common bit sizes

Most screwdriver bits are named by their **shank**, the hex-shaped end that goes into the handle.

| | Standard driver bits | Precision bits |
|---|---|---|
| Shank size | **1/4 inch** hex (about 6.35mm) | **4mm** hex |
| Where you see them | Multi-bit screwdrivers, bit sets, impact and drill bit holders | Electronics, phone, laptop, eyeglass, and hobby repair kits |
| Typical tips | Phillips #1–#3, flat, square, larger Torx and hex | Pentalobe, Tri-Wing, tri-point, Torx T3–T10, Phillips 000–1, small flat and hex |
| Handle | Full-size and grippy | Thin and pen-sized |

The tips on precision bits are what make them useful: they match the tiny, often unusual screws inside modern gadgets. The shank is just small enough that it won't fit the screwdriver most of us actually like using.

## Why that's annoying

Precision kit handles are skinny so they can spin fast between your fingers. That's fine for loose screws. It's a different story when a screw is tight from the factory, stuck with threadlocker, or sitting at the bottom of a deep hole. A thin handle gives you little leverage and makes it easy to slip, and a slipped bit can strip a tiny screw head in one bad turn.

Most "human evolution" resources are one of two things: a beautiful encyclopedia you read, or an animated arrow sweeping out of Africa. Both are useful. Neither lets you **watch the actual evidence appear**, find by find, where it was buried and when.

That's what [Human Journey](https://humanjourney.zinglab.com/?ref=zinglab-blog) does.

## The contenders

- **[Human Journey](https://humanjourney.zinglab.com/?ref=zinglab-blog)** — ZingLab's interactive globe of the physical human record.

## Why Human Journey wins

### 1. It shows evidence, not just arrows

Each point is a real find, placed where it was found and timed to when it was **deposited**, not when it was dug up. Watch the record fill in, and you see where the evidence is dense, where it's thin, and how much of our picture rests on a few famous sites.

### 2. Time that feels like time

Switch from **Even** pacing to **True scale** and the playback moves at a constant rate through the years. Hundreds of thousands of years pass with a scattered flicker — then the last few millennia arrive in a flood. It's the most intuitive lesson in deep time we know of.

### 3. Live, open data

Human Journey pulls live records from the **Paleobiology Database**, with a saved snapshot as a fallback, plus curated landmarks — art, tools, footprints, and famous finds — compiled from published literature. Everything is attributed.

### 4. Bring your own dataset

Teachers, students, and researchers can **load any CSV** with coordinates and ages. Map a class project, a site survey, or a filtered PBDB download in seconds.

### 5. Honest about uncertainty

Each entry shows its **age range**, and the app explains why: radiocarbon fades out around 50,000 years ago, and older dates come from methods with wide error bars. It even reminds you that the coastlines are today's — sea levels were much lower for most of this period.

### 6. Readable at a glance

Points run from **red (oldest) to blue (most recent)**, and each site casts a faint 2,000 km glow, so regional hotspots emerge on their own. Jump straight to 50,000, 100,000, 250,000, 500,000 or 1,000,000 years ago.

## Where the competition is genuinely good

- **Smithsonian Human Origins** is the best place to **read** about each hominin species, with expert-written profiles and images. Use it alongside Human Journey.
- **Journey of Mankind** tells the **genetic migration story** clearly as a guided animation.
- **Ancient Earth** is wonderful for **plate tectonics** and the deep time before humans existed.

## Classroom idea (15 minutes)

1. Open [Human Journey](https://humanjourney.zinglab.com/?ref=zinglab-blog) on a projector in **Even** mode and press play.
2. Pause at a hotspot. Ask: why so many finds here? (Preservation? Excavation history? Population?)
3. Switch to **True scale** and play again. Ask: what does this say about how recent most of the record is?
4. Have students look up one find in the Smithsonian's species pages.
